
# AstroBot Scientific Knowledge Base
## SPHEREx — Verified Mission Facts

Status: Reference knowledge, verified against NASA/JPL and the SPHEREx science team.
Purpose: Supply factual grounding for AstroBot's scientific answers.

### 1. Mission Identity

Name: SPHEREx

Full form: Spectro-Photometer for the History of the Universe, Epoch of Reionization and Ices Explorer.

Agency: NASA
Launch date: March 11, 2025
Mission type: Space-based astronomical observatory.

SPHEREx conducts an all-sky spectral survey primarily in infrared wavelengths.

### 2. Primary Scientific Objectives

SPHEREx has three primary scientific goals:

1. COSMIC INFLATION
Study the physics of inflation by measuring the three-dimensional large-scale distribution of galaxies.

2. GALAXY FORMATION HISTORY
Trace the history of galactic light production, including the collective light from faint, distant, and otherwise unresolved galaxies.

3. WATER AND BIOGENIC ICES
Investigate the abundance and distribution of water, carbon dioxide, and other biogenic ices in regions where stars and planetary systems form.

### 3. Observational Capabilities

Wavelength coverage: Approximately 0.75–5.0 micrometres.

Spectral bands: 102 infrared wavelengths.

Survey strategy: Four planned all-sky surveys during the nominal mission.

The mission measures spectra to investigate astronomical objects' properties, compositions, and distances.

### 4. Scientific Boundaries

SPHEREx is not primarily an exoplanet atmospheric biosignature detection mission.

SPHEREx does not directly establish the presence of extraterrestrial life.

Infrared light is not the same as ultraviolet light.

A spectral observation is not automatically evidence of an astronomical transient.

### 5. AstroShift Application Context

AstroShift investigates changes between astronomical observations.

For reliable comparison, observations must be evaluated for:
- Sky-coordinate alignment
- Wavelength or spectral-band compatibility
- Observation timestamps
- Instrumental effects
- Background and noise differences

A detected image difference is a candidate anomaly, not automatically a confirmed astronomical discovery.

These AstroShift rules are analysis safeguards, not claims about SPHEREx's official primary objectives.

### 8. Related Space Telescope Capabilities

NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope:
- Successfully launched on August 30, 2026.
- Its Coronagraph Instrument is a technology
  demonstration for directly imaging exoplanets.
- The coronagraph includes imaging and spectroscopy
  capabilities, but should not be described as an
  established oxygen-biosignature detection system.
- Do not describe Roman as awaiting launch.

NASA's James Webb Space Telescope (JWST):
- Can use transmission spectroscopy to investigate
  the chemical composition of exoplanet atmospheres.
- Atmospheric characterization is not equivalent
  to confirmed detection of extraterrestrial life.

Scientific distinction:
- Transmission spectroscopy and direct imaging
  are different observing techniques.
- Do not treat JWST and Roman as interchangeable.
- When answering SPHEREx-specific questions,
  avoid unnecessary claims about other missions.

### 9. AstroShift Image Differencing — Scientific Validation

Purpose:
Provide scientifically responsible guidance when comparing
SPHEREx observations and evaluating candidate anomalies.

#### Space-Based Instrument Effects

SPHEREx observes from space.

Do not describe terrestrial atmospheric seeing or
atmospheric turbulence as a source of distortion
in SPHEREx's own space-based images.

Relevant effects may include:
- Detector noise and cosmic ray contamination
- Optical point-spread-function variations
- Spacecraft pointing and image registration errors
- Photometric calibration differences
- Zodiacal and other infrared background variations
- Data processing and subtraction artefacts

#### Spectral Compatibility

SPHEREx uses linear variable filters.

Calibrated image positions have associated
sky coordinates and wavelength information.

Before subtracting observations, verify:
- Overlapping sky coverage
- Compatible effective wavelengths or spectral channels
- Suitable photometric calibration and units
- Observation timestamps
- Comparable spatial resolution or PSF treatment

Never interpret differences between incompatible
wavelength measurements as proof of temporal variability.

#### Image Registration

Astronomical images do not need mathematically
perfect alignment.

Use appropriate astrometric registration and assess
residual alignment uncertainty relative to the
image resolution and analysis requirements.

Poor registration can create artificial residuals
around otherwise unchanged sources.

#### Candidate Anomaly Validation

A difference image identifies potential changes,
not automatically new astronomical objects.

Evaluate:
- Statistical significance and measurement uncertainty
- Background noise and detector artefacts
- PSF and calibration differences
- Known objects and catalog matches
- Additional observations where available

Parallax and proper-motion checks may be useful
for certain nearby or moving objects, but are not
universal requirements for every candidate anomaly.

#### Scientific Reporting

Separate:
OBSERVATION — Directly supported measurements.
INFERENCE — Possible physical explanations.
UNCERTAINTY — What remains unverified.

Never invent source coordinates, flux measurements,
detection significance, or follow-up observations.

Do not claim to have processed observational data
unless actual processing was performed.
